Modern Ceiling Report — the post-ban format

What your deck does in good hands

A deck's average win rate is a fact about everyone who sleeved it up, most of whom are not you. This report separates a deck's base rate from its ceiling — the win rate a strong pilot can expect — by modelling pilot skill instead of reading it off the leaderboard.

Why the obvious version of this doesn't work

Over a 15-round event a single pilot's win rate carries a standard error near 13 points. Rank decks by their best pilot and you mostly rank them by how many pilots rolled dice — a 6-pilot deck beats a 60-pilot deck almost every time.

So the ceiling here is the 90th percentile of a fitted distribution of true pilot skill (hierarchical beta-binomial, pooled across all four events), not the 90th percentile of observed rates. On simulated data where the truth is known, the naive version overstates the ceiling by 8–12 points; on this data it overstates by 13–16. Both numbers are in the table so you can see the gap.

So the slot map is the trustworthy artifact. Anything that does clear significance is either an enormous effect or a build split masquerading as a card choice — and stronger players converge on correct builds, so a winning card may simply be the card good players pick. Nothing here separates those.

How this was built

Every number traces to melee.gg's own published pairings. Nothing is hand-entered.

Classification

Melee's deck name is free text the player typed, and when they leave it blank melee auto-fills a colour string. Across these events that produced — distinct name strings for roughly 30 real archetypes, with entries like "Izzet", "W-U-B-G" and "Colorless" carrying no information at all.

So decks are classified on card signatures, not names. Signatures were mined from the decklists themselves rather than written from memory — the current format contains cards no prior knowledge would include.

The ceiling model

For each deck, pilot i's true win rate is drawn from a Beta, and their observed record is Binomial on it:

pi ~ Beta(μ·κ, (1−μ)·κ)
wi ~ Binomial(mi, pi)

μ is the deck's mean true win rate; κ is concentration — a low κ means the deck's outcome depends heavily on its pilot. Ceiling is the 90th percentile of that latent Beta. κ is poorly identified from few pilots, so each deck's κ is shrunk toward a global κ fitted across all 3,383 pilots. Confidence intervals come from a 300-fold bootstrap over pilots.

Why the window starts at a ban

The boundary was found in the data rather than assumed. Phlage, Titan of Fire\u2019s Fury appears in 25–34% of Modern decks from January through 17 May 2026, then in 0 of 28 decks on 24 May and 0 of the several hundred that follow. Nothing else disappears like that.

Everything before that line is a different format and is excluded, however much data it would have added.

Recency and event tier

Matches are weighted by age on a 60-day half-life, then renormalised to mean 1. Without the renormalisation the model reads uniformly smaller weights as less evidence, drives kappa up, and compresses every ceiling for a purely arithmetic reason — in testing, halving all weights moved kappa from 41 to 140 while leaving the mean untouched.

Events under 100 players are excluded. Pooling 40-player local RCQs barely moves the win rates but inflates every ceiling by about 4 points, because a wider pilot pool reads as a wider latent skill spread. Those 72 small events carried only 19% of the matches, so dropping them costs little and keeps the ceiling meaning what you want it to mean.

What this cannot tell you

Pilots are self-selected, not randomly assigned. A deck with a wide fitted spread may genuinely reward skill, or may simply have attracted a wider mix of players. This data cannot separate those two, and no amount of extra events will fix it — it would take randomising who plays what.

Treat a wide spread as "this deck's result varied a lot by pilot", which is true and useful, rather than "this deck rewards skill", which is a causal claim the design does not support.
